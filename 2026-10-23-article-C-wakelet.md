That date stamped on the bottom of your supplement bottle is not like the date on a carton of milk. Supplements rarely become dangerous the day after expiry — but their potency can decline, sometimes well before the printed date if the bottle has been stored badly. Understanding what the date means, and what degrades a product faster, will save you money and disappointment.

What the date actually guarantees

For most supplements the printed date is a "best before" marker: the manufacturer's assurance that the product retains its labelled potency up to that point when stored as directed. Vitamins, probiotics, and fish oils are the most sensitive categories. Probiotics lose live cultures over time, especially in warmth; fish oils can oxidise and turn rancid; vitamin C degrades with heat, light, and air exposure. Minerals, by contrast, are generally stable for years.

Storage matters more than shoppers think

A bathroom cabinet — warm, humid, steamy — is one of the worst places for supplements despite being where most people keep them. Heat accelerates degradation, moisture can break down tablets and capsules, and light damages light-sensitive vitamins. A cool, dry cupboard away from the cooker is better; the refrigerator suits probiotics and opened fish oils unless the label says otherwise. And always reseal the bottle: every opening admits humid air, and those little silica packets are doing real work.

Three checks before you swallow

1. Read the storage instructions and be honest about whether you have followed them. A bottle kept above the hob for a year is not the product the manufacturer tested.
2. Use your senses. Rancid fish oil smells strongly fishy (fresh oil should not), discoloured or crumbly tablets suggest moisture damage, and clumped powders have absorbed water.
3. Check the format's weak points. Softgels can leak or stick together with age; gummies can harden; liquids are the most perishable format of all.
